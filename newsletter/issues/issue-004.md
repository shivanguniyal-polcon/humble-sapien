## Lead

Take a citizen, a card, and a hospital she has never walked into. Between those three things sits a chain of about six steps, and each one can either deliver the promise or quietly break it. This month we trace that chain end to end for Ayushman Bharat PM-JAY, because the scheme's headline numbers — 40 crore cards, nine crore admissions — tell you the promise is being handed out, but not whether it survives the walk from the card to the ward.

## Touchpoints

- Eligibility: The SECC deprivation database decides who is eligible. If you were not counted correctly in that survey, the scheme does not know you exist — and there is no appeal at this step.
- Enrollment: Eligible households are offered a card. Getting one can mean a camp, a queue, or a Common Service Centre. A card is not automatic; someone has to generate it against your name.
- Discovery: The patient must find an empanelled hospital, and know that she is entitled to go without paying. Awareness is the weakest link in most states — the card in a drawer treats nobody.
- Admission: At the hospital desk, the card is verified against the hospital's own system. This is where a mismatched name, a missing mobile number or an expired verification turns a cashless promise into a demand for money up front.
- Treatment and discharge: Care is delivered against the scheme's package rates. The package defines what is covered — and, just as importantly, what is not, which is where "cashless" meets its limits.
- Grievance: If a hospital refuses, or a claim is denied, there is a helpline (14555) and the CGRMS portal. Whether a grievance moves depends on whether the complainant knows the route and can afford to walk it.

## Plain language

The entitlement clause is written the way most entitlement clauses are: in the passive, with a noun stack that takes two passes to parse. Read it once as a citizen and you would be forgiven for not knowing whether you have to pay anything. Read the rewrite and the promise is unmistakable — no cash at the counter, any empanelled hospital, any state. The simplification removes nothing; it only moves the phrasing from officer to citizen. Both texts always print together, because simplification can smooth a nuance, and the reader deserves to see the original.

## Stakeholders

Cardholding households are the clear beneficiaries: cashless secondary and tertiary care at the point of need, which for a poor family is the difference between an illness and a debt. Empanelled hospitals gain a steady, if slow-paying, patient flow at a defined price — 31,005 of them by March 2025, up from 18,236 in 2018. Who pays is the general taxpayer, through central and state budgets that funded ₹1,29,386 crore of treatment by March 2025; hospitals additionally carry the float while claims settle.

And who is missing is the hardest column, and the one most often skipped. The scheme sees enrolment and admissions, so it cannot see the person never enumerated into it. Without a card, without awareness, without the documents or the language or the distance to an empanelled hospital, a citizen can be fully eligible and never appear in a single PM-JAY statistic. That absence is not a data gap to be cleaned up later. It is the population the promise was written for.

## Field note

The most useful thing we learned building this: the grievance step is where the whole scheme's honesty is tested, and it is the step with the least instrumentation. Claims data tells you how many people got treated. It does not tell you how many were turned away at the desk and went home. That number, if it exists anywhere, is the one worth publishing next.
